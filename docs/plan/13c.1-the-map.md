# Item 13c.1 — The map, and the things that move on it

**Objective.** Start from the tile and the vehicle; let everything else be a read of them.

A rectangular grid of tiles, each a declared number of kilometres on a side, and **each carrying
its own drawn characteristics**: the place it is in, the mix of water, plain, hill, mountain and
forest it is made of, how high it stands, and how much of every declared resource is in it. Those
are TECHNOLOGY primitives — the world, not an outcome — and everything economic is a read over
them. **Every tile belongs to exactly one PLACE**, a land **region** or a **sea area**, so there is
nowhere a thing can be that is nowhere. Places group into **countries**, and a country — not a
region — is what has a currency, a central bank and a treasury.

A vehicle is **at a place** and can be **on a voyage**, and a voyage is somewhere: it has a path of
tiles, a distance travelled along it, and therefore a position you can point at. Each period it
advances by what the weather **at the place it is actually in** let it do. So a gale in one sea area
holds up the ships in that sea area and no others; takes down a share of the hulls caught in it, with
the cargo aboard, on two named owners' books; and leaves the next freight session with fewer free
hulls near the trade — which is Freight B2.a's "violent and inelastic in the short run" as a
mechanism instead of a claim.

**Capacity stops being a declared number.** There is no capacity per route. There are hulls, and
they are somewhere: a carrier can offer on a route only what it has free **at that origin**, and
getting a hull to an origin is itself a voyage, in ballast, that costs time and wear. Routes become
distinct (A4) because a hull in one sea cannot serve another, and capacity is short-run fixed (B2)
for a second reason beside the build lag.

**Still no freight rate anywhere.** Distance, terrain and weather set a carrier's COST; the session
sets the price (Law 3). And nothing caps what a place can hold: the ground gets worse as more of it
is worked, which is what a rent is (Law 6).

**Read first.** §38 Freight A1–A4, A3.a, B1–B4, B2.a, C1–C3, D1–D6, D3.a, E1–E3 — built at 13c

## Why this is inserted before the rest of 13c

13c's remaining steps measure DISTANCE — the location basis and a blocked route (D3, D3.a, D5),
"source locally" (C2), the price indices with freight in them (Indices D4). This world has one
producing place, and `seeds/foundation.ts:222` declares `transitPeriods: 4,
unitsPerVesselPerPeriod: 25000, sailsIn: 4` on every leg. Each of those tests would measure nothing
and be rewritten the day the map landed. The dependency runs one way: the map needs nothing from the
rest of 13c.

## The four decisions this item takes

### 1. A TILE IS A BUNDLE OF PRIMITIVES, and they are drawn

The physical world is the canonical TECHNOLOGY primitive (Law 2: real-world primitives may be
imported; it is real-world *equilibria* that may not). A tile is not a thin index into a lookup —
**it carries its own characteristics, every one of them drawn and every one of them declared**:

| on every tile | what it is | kind |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **place** | the region or sea area it belongs to | drawn |
| **terrain mix** | the share of it that is water, plain, hill, mountain, forest — summing to one | drawn |
| **elevation** | metres above the water | drawn |
| **resources** | how much of each declared resource is in or on it | drawn |

These are not derived from each other and they are not outcomes. They are the world, and the
economy is a thing that happens on top of them.

**A terrain MIX rather than a type**, because a fifty-kilometre square is not one thing and because
a mix removes every threshold from what follows: crossing a tile costs `Σ share / kmPerDay` (you
have to cross all of it), what gets through a gale is the share-weighted survival, what a hectare
yields is the share-weighted quality. Nothing steps at a boundary and nothing is clamped (Law 6).
Water is a terrain kind like the others: a tile that is part water is a marsh to a lorry and a
shallow to a hull, and both are slow — which is what a coast actually is.

**A tile's place is the discrete fact and it is drawn too.** Every tile belongs to exactly one
place, and a place is a land region or a sea area. That is what lets weather reach a ship: weather
is published per place, so a thing at sea has to be IN somewhere or nothing can happen to it. A
coast is a region tile touching a sea-area tile — read off the places, which are primitives, not off
a flag that could disagree with them.

### 1a. Resources are drawn for every tile, whether or not anything eats them yet

`RESOURCES` is a table and **adding one is one row**:

```
export interface ResourceDecl {
  readonly id: ResourceId;
  readonly name: string;
  readonly unit: string;                  // what a tile's endowment is counted in
  readonly clumping: ParamId;             // deposits or spread: how correlated the draw is
  readonly inTerrain: Record<string, ParamId>;   // how much a tile of each terrain tends to hold
  readonly why: string;
}
```

An assembly fault requires an `inTerrain` entry for **every** declared terrain, so a resource added
without saying where it is found is refused at assembly rather than found later as a hole.

**Every resource is drawn on every tile from the first world, including the ones no recipe consumes
yet**, and that is deliberate rather than sloppy. The map must be STABLE: if adding a line that
smelts ore re-drew the world, the world would not be a world — the same seed would mean a different
place every time the economy grew, and no run would be comparable with any other. Oil is under the
ground before anybody drills it. A resource with no line on it yet is not a display-only number
(Appendix B is about a SURFACE that changes the model); it is the world being larger than the
economy, which is the normal condition of a world and the reason there is anything to discover.

### 1b. Favourability is the READ, and it is what makes an industry sit where it sits

The primitives are on the tile; what an industry thinks of a place is arithmetic over them, computed
where it is used and never stored:

| the read | what it is over | who asks |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **what a hectare yields** | the resource here, times the terrain's quality for it | `firms/produce.ts` — into the same exponent the season enters |
| **what it costs to build here** | the share-weighted buildability of the terrain mix | 13c.2's project, per candidate place |
| **how fast a voyage crosses** | `Σ share / kmPerDay` over the mix | the sailing phase |
| **what gets through a gale** | share-weighted `exp(-(wind / standsWind) ^ hardness)` | the sailing phase |
| **where a port can be** | a region tile touching a sea-area tile | leg generation |

So "flat and easy country is cheap to build on" and "an oil-rich place grows the industry that uses
oil" are both OUTCOMES of a firm's own decision reading the ground — never a rule, never a score,
never a coefficient somebody weighted (13c.2's guard).

### 1c. Why industry should cluster, and what would prove it does not

The intended behaviour is that **industry gathers where the freight it saves is worth more than the
ground it bids up, and hugs the coast where it exports** — and that this happens dynamically, out of
decisions, never out of a rule. Two forces do it, and neither is a coefficient:

**What pulls together — freight.** A mill near its grain pays less to get it; a bakery near its
buyers pays less to deliver. Both are the same freight print, already cleared on a real leg. And a
port is worth being near for a reason that needs no new mechanism: **a cargo is a real holding at
every place it reaches**, so an inland exporter simply buys TWO legs — road to the coast, sea from
it — where a coastal one buys one. Transshipment is not a feature to build; it falls out of legs
being per-pair and cargo being units somebody owns. The second leg costs a second freight, a second
wait and a second session that can fail to clear, and that is exactly what coastal ground is worth.

**What pushes apart — rent and the wage.** The marginal hectare of a region falls as more of it is
worked, so the next firm there yields less than the last. And a region's labour venue is one book:
more employers bidding for the same hours clears at a higher wage. Both are prices, both already
clear, and between them they are why everything does not pile onto one tile — without a cap
anywhere (Law 6).

**What is NOT here, and must not appear**: no distance-decay term, no gravity equation, no
agglomeration elasticity, no "attractiveness" score. The pull is a freight print and the push is a
rent and a wage, and a firm comparing places is comparing money (13c.2).

**Law 17 — the forecast and its killer.** "Industry will cluster and hug the coast" is a forecast,
so it is worth nothing without the test that would falsify it. Three, and each is a scenario 16 can
run rather than a number to tune:

1. **Flatten the freight.** Give every leg the same trivial cost. If the clustering survives that,
   it is not coming from transport and something else is producing it — a defect.
2. **Flatten the ground.** Draw a world whose tiles are identical. If firms still pile into one
   region, the dispersion force is missing or the rent walk is wrong.
3. **Landlock the exporter.** Move the foreign buyer so every route needs two legs. If the coastal
   premium does not fall to nothing, it was not the extra leg that made it.

If the world does not cluster, that is a FINDING about the mechanism — most likely a missing one —
and not a licence to add a term that makes it (Law 11, Law 12).

### 2. A region is a PLACE; a country is a MONEY

`RegionDecl` does two jobs at once. They separate: **`CountryDecl {id, name, ccy}`** takes the
currency, the central bank, the treasury, the sovereign line, the FX pair and the equity index;
**`RegionDecl {id, name, country}`** keeps the place, and `ccy` is **deleted** for
`registry.currencyOf(region)`.

This is the cheap direction, measured: the economy is already written per region — `goodId`,
`goodMarketId`, `GoodTerms.region`, `plantVintageId`, the `region` cell-key dimension,
`PartyBase.region`, and **one labour venue per (region, occupation)** — so making regions smaller
makes all of it per-place with no renaming. The 176 `.region` sites do not move; the ~25 that spell
`registry.region(x).ccy` do.

Seed B3 and Currency B1 stay literally true (a region determines its money, through its country).
Cross-Border and Indices D1 mean currency area; they are re-read as **country**, COVERAGE says so,
and 13i gets the note.

### 3. A vehicle is somewhere, and a voyage is state with one writer

A hull is plant, and `plantVintageId(kind, region, serviceDate)` already keys plant by place — so
"a hull at Bergen" and "a hull at Lisbon" are already two instruments and already not fungible. What
is new is the **voyage**: a kernel store, the shape 13a's contract store already set, written only by
settlement instructions so no module writes it.

```
Voyage = { id, carrier, shipper, path: PlaceId[], tilePath, kmTravelled,
           cargoLots: LotId[], hulls: LienId, freightAgreed, departed }
```

- **Position is tile-exact**: walk `tilePath` to `kmTravelled`. The place it is in is a read of that
  tile. The picture draws it there; the weather finds it there. Same fact, one writer.
- **The hull is LIENED**, not moved: `register.encumber` binds it for the voyage, and the register
  already refuses to move encumbered units. A hull cannot be sold, sent on a second voyage, or
  counted as capacity twice — with no new rule written (Law 12: the fix that removes code).
- **The cargo stays the shipper's** the whole time, as units of an in-transit instrument, and the
  voyage names the **lots** it carries. Two facts, two writers: the register owns how many and
  whose, the voyage owns where they are. A3.a's working capital is real: the shipper has paid and
  has not got.

### 4. Capacity is an OUTCOME of where the hulls are

`RouteDecl.transitPeriods`, `.unitsPerVesselPerPeriod`, `.sailsIn`, `.sailsHardness` are **all four
deleted**, and `ROUTES` with them. A route is a pair of land regions and nothing else (A4: "a pair
of places and a time"). What a carrier can offer on it is what it has **free at that origin**,
which is a register read. A hull in the wrong place must sail there empty first — **ballast**, a
voyage with no cargo, costing days and wear — which is what makes capacity slow to answer a shortage
(B2) and is also what stops every hull ending up at the destination and the world seizing.

## Design

### Kernel: `registry/geography.ts`

Two DATA tables and one drawn world. The tables are the registry's (Law 15); every number on them is
a declared parameter; no mechanism branches on a terrain or a resource — they read these.

```
export type PlaceId    = RegionId | SeaAreaId;   // the union, so a region goes where a place is wanted
export type TileIndex  = Brand<number, 'TileIndex'>;
export type TerrainId  = Brand<string, 'TerrainId'>;
export type ResourceId = Brand<string, 'ResourceId'>;

export interface TerrainDecl {           // WHAT THE GROUND IS LIKE TO CROSS AND TO BUILD ON
  readonly id: TerrainId;                // water | plain | hill | mountain | forest
  readonly name: string;
  readonly kmPerDay: ParamId;            // how far a loaded carrier gets across it in a day
  readonly buildKm2: ParamId;            // what erecting a unit of plant on it takes, per km2
  readonly standsWind: ParamId;          // Freight B4: what a passage over it stands ...
  readonly windHardness: ParamId;        //             ... and how sharply it stops standing it
  readonly carries: readonly string[];   // the capital kinds that move over it: hulls, vehicles
  readonly why: string;
}

export interface ResourceDecl {          // WHAT IS IN OR ON THE GROUND
  readonly id: ResourceId;               // arable | timber | ore | oil | ...
  readonly name: string;
  readonly unit: string;
  readonly clumping: ParamId;            // deposits or spread: how correlated the draw is
  readonly inTerrain: Readonly<Record<string, ParamId>>;   // one entry per declared terrain
  readonly why: string;
}

export interface GeographyDecl {         // THE DRAWN WORLD. Written once, by the draw.
  readonly tileKm: number;               // RESOLUTION (Law 2)
  readonly cols: number; readonly rows: number;
  readonly terrains: readonly TerrainDecl[];
  readonly resources: readonly ResourceDecl[];
  readonly places: readonly PlaceId[];
  readonly seaAreas: readonly SeaAreaDecl[];
  readonly place: Int16Array;                             // per tile: exactly one
  readonly elevation: Float64Array;                       // per tile: metres above the water
  readonly mix: ReadonlyMap<TerrainId, Float64Array>;     // per tile: shares, summing to one
  readonly deposit: ReadonlyMap<ResourceId, Float64Array>;// per tile: how much is here
}
```

`mix` and `deposit` are **maps keyed by the declared id**, not parallel arrays: adding a terrain or a
resource is one table row and one layer, and nothing silently mis-indexes.

Reads, all pure functions over the above:

- `placeAt(g, tile)`, `tilesOf(g, place)`, `areaKm2(g, place)`
- `touches(g, a, b)` — two places share a tile **edge**; a shared corner is not a border
- `coastOf(g, region)` — its tiles that touch a sea area's tile, which is where a port can be
- `crossKm2(g, tile, kind)` — `Σ share / kmPerDay` over the mix: you have to cross all of a tile
- `standsIn(g, params, tile)` — the share-weighted wind a passage over this tile stands
- `buildOn(g, params, tile)` — the share-weighted cost of erecting plant here
- `depositAt(g, resource, tile)`, `yieldOf(g, params, resource, tile)` — what is here, times what
  this mix of ground does for it
- `path(g, params, from, to, by): Path | undefined` — the least-**day** walk, 8-neighbour (a
  traveller cuts a corner though a border does not), over tiles the capital kind `by` can cross;
  `Path = { tiles, km, days, places, kmPerPlace }`

Paths between places are computed once at assembly and held beside the grid: the map never moves,
the draw is its one writer, and `path()` is the only thing that can produce one (Law 4, Law 18).

### Kernel: `world/voyages.ts` (new store)

Rows as above. Written only by instructions applied by settlement. Reads: the voyages a party is on
either side of, the voyages currently in a place, a hull's voyage. Contract violations: a voyage with
no path, cargo not held by its shipper, hulls not liened, `kmTravelled` past the path's length.

### Movement, and what the weather does to it

One phase, `freight.sail`, at cycle 0 before `corporateActions`. Per voyage, in order:

1. **Where it is.** `placeAt(tile at kmTravelled)`.
2. **What it made.** `advance = kmPerDay(terrain under it) * periodDays * passable`, where
   `passable = exp(-(wind(place, period) / standsWind) ^ hardness)` — the same arithmetic 13c
   already uses for a storm on land plant, positive at every wind and never one, so a voyage in a
   gale crawls and never stops dead and nothing is clamped (Law 6).
3. **What it lost.** The same wind takes `1 - exp(-(wind / standsWind(kind)) ^ hardness)` of the
   hulls on the voyage, **and the cargo they were carrying goes with them** — destroy legs on two
   named holders, the carrier's plant and the shipper's goods, through the same `capital.weathered`
   journal 13c already writes. A loss at sea is not a write-down; it is units gone from named books.
4. **Whether it arrived.** `kmTravelled >= path.km` → the cargo reseats to the destination at what
   it cost **including the voyage** (D2), the lien releases, and the hulls are now free **at the
   destination**, which is where they actually are.

The weather is the one event 13c already publishes; this is its fifth consequence, and it reads it
through the kernel read, never an import (4.9b).

### Distance, time and cost

- A step to an edge neighbour is `tileKm`; to a corner neighbour `tileKm * sqrt(2)`. A path's `km`
  is the sum.
- **There is no `transitPeriods`.** How long a voyage takes is how long it takes — an outcome of its
  distance, the ground under it, and the weather it met. A calm crossing is faster than a rough one,
  and neither is declared.
- **Cost, and only cost.** The hull is used up **per unit-kilometre** (`wearPerUnitKm`); the crew is
  paid **per day** through the carrier's existing labour scale, and a slow passage is a dear one
  because the crew is aboard longer. A carrier posts at what the voyage will cost IT; the session
  clears. 13c's test that greps the register for a freight rate stays and gains the new params.

### The ground reaches the yield, and nothing is capped

`Recipe.standsOn: ResourceId | null` — null for a line made indoors, exactly as an empty `exposedTo`
is. What a region offers is a walk of its tiles **best first**, and the answer is the yield of the
**marginal** hectare at the area already in use; past the last tile the same decline continues by the
ratio the last two set, which is what working the same ground harder means. So there is **no cap** —
a region never refuses plant — the return from the next unit falls continuously and **never reaches
zero**, and what stops expansion is the hurdle rate refusing a project, which is a decision a firm
already makes (13c.2). Rent emerges instead of being assumed.

Area in use is `plantUnits * landPerUnit`, read over the register at the produce site where the
season is already read, never stored. One formula, one site:
`pow(tech.yieldRate, 1 / (season * ground))`.

### The draw: `seeds/map.ts` (new)

Pure, with its own labelled stream — `prng(seed, 'geography')`, as `drawClimate` has
`prng(seed, 'environment')`. It runs in `foundation.ts` **before** assembly, because the places it
draws are registry declarations. It is not a module: the map is structure, and nothing in the period
loop writes it. Every step below writes a PRIMITIVE; none of them derives one from another.

1. **Elevation.** `cols = round(worldWidthKm / tileKm)`; a value-noise field, in metres. The lowest
   `oceanShare` of it is below the water, which is how the coastline comes out of the draw rather
   than out of a number somebody set.
2. **Channels.** Drawn water walks edge to edge, and the outer ring is water so nothing runs off the
   world. This is what splits the land: whether two countries share a coastline or an ocean is an
   **outcome** of the draw, not a setting.
3. **Terrain mix.** Per tile, over the declared kinds, summing to one: water below the line, and
   above it a mix from elevation, local slope and its own correlated noise — so mountains come in
   ranges, forests in stands, and a coastal tile is genuinely part water.
4. **Resources.** One layer per row of `RESOURCES`, each with its own correlated noise at its own
   `clumping` and scaled by what its `inTerrain` says this mix tends to hold. **Every declared
   resource is drawn on every tile**, consumed or not — see 1a.
5. **Places.** Land regions by randomised-frontier growth from seed tiles; sea areas by the same
   growth over the water. Every tile ends in exactly one place.
6. **Countries** by grouping contiguous regions. An island country is a legitimate outcome.
7. **Names** from a syllable table. Ids stay `us.3`; an internal id is never a display name (Law 9).

### The climate is drawn on the map

Memory and width per fact are drawn from the grid rather than per place independently, so neighbours
have similar climates and a sea area's weather is like its neighbours'. The period's departure stays
independent per place; **correlated weather losses across places are named to 13h**, where the
insurer that cares whether two claims are one event lives.

### Audit contributions

One new identity, in the existing units family: **every unit that left an origin is in a voyage, at a
destination, or destroyed by a named event** — per good, per period, exactly, from the ledger. That
is E3 ("no goods in transit owned by nobody") made checkable now that in-transit units have a place
and a way to be lost. The map's own invariants — every tile in exactly one place, every place
contiguous, no land region on the frame, a country with no regions — are **assembly-time contract
violations**, because a world whose geometry is broken cannot run.

## Steps

- [x] **A country is what has a money; a region is a place.** `CountryDecl`; `RegionDecl.country`;
      `RegionDecl.ccy` deleted for `registry.currencyOf(region)` at the eighty sites that spelt it;
      `PlaceId` as the union of `RegionId` and `SeaAreaId`; the first assembly-time violations. Done
      — but the tile shape it sketched was wrong and step 2 replaces it: it made terrain a thin index
      and treated the ground as something to be minimised, when **a tile's characteristics are Law 2
      TECHNOLOGY primitives** and the physical world is the thing that law exists to let in
- [x] **A tile carries its own characteristics, and they are tables.** `TerrainDecl` (kmPerDay,
      buildKm2, standsWind, windHardness, what it carries) and `ResourceDecl` (unit, clumping,
      inTerrain) as registry tables; `GeographyDecl` holding `place`, `elevation`, a terrain `mix`
      and one `deposit` layer per resource, both as **maps keyed by the declared id** so adding a
      terrain or a resource is one row and one layer and nothing mis-indexes. Assembly faults: a
      resource with no `inTerrain` entry for some declared terrain; a mix that does not sum to one;
      a tile with no place. Tests: the fault fires when a row is added without saying where the
      thing is found — which is the guard that makes the table safe to extend
- [x] **The draw, and it is deterministic.** `seeds/map.ts`: elevation, channels, the terrain mix,
      one resource layer per declared row, places by randomised-frontier growth over land AND water,
      countries by grouping. Tests: same seed same world, byte for byte; every tile in exactly one
      place; every place contiguous; water on all four borders; every tile's mix sums to one; every
      declared resource has a layer **whether or not a recipe consumes it** — and a test that adding
      a resource row changes no other layer, because the map must be stable or no two runs compare
- [x] **Kilometres and days, and the resolution is tested by invariance.** `path()` as the least-day
      8-neighbour walk with `km`, `tiles`, `places` and `kmPerPlace` out of it; `touches` on shared
      edges only; `coastOf`. Tests: distance symmetric and respecting the triangle inequality;
      **a fixture coastline stated in km, drawn at `tileKm` and `tileKm / 2`, gives the same path
      length to derived dust** — which is what makes `tileKm` a RESOLUTION and not a tuned number
      (Law 2); the same km across mountains takes more days than across plain
- [x] **The seed draws the world and places what is already in it.** `ABROAD` becomes countries with
      regions under them; central bank, treasury, sovereign paper, FX pair and equity index re-point
      to the country; the firms are placed on ground that suits them; household cells keep the
      `region` key dimension, which now names a place; **a labour venue per (region, occupation)
      appears per place with no code change, so the wage differs by place from period one**. Tests:
      one country per region; every party's money is its region's country's; a grep test that no
      `.ccy` is read off a `RegionDecl`

## Exit criteria

Two regions produce the same good at different prices and the gap is what somebody paid to move it;
a gale in one sea area holds up the ships in that sea area, sinks some of them with their cargo, and
leaves the next session short of hulls — and the freight price moves violently because the hulls are
gone, not because a number was raised; a hull is where it actually is and cannot be in two trades at
once; a carrier sails a hull back empty because that is where it is worth most; a farm on better
ground makes more, the next farm makes less, and no region anywhere refuses to be built on; no
freight rate exists in the register; halving the tile does not move a distance in kilometres.

## Guard

Freight E1 (no instantaneous, costless transport), E2 (no shipment without capacity, and no capacity
without a carrier that owns it — now enforced by a lien rather than a rule), E3 (no goods in transit
owned by nobody), A4, B2.a; Law 2 (`tileKm` is a RESOLUTION and the invariance test says so); Law 3
(no declared freight rate); Law 4 (`ccy` has one writer; the register owns how many, the voyage owns
where); Law 6 (no cap on what a place holds; progress is positive at every wind); Law 15 (terrain and
resources are registry TABLES and every number on them is a parameter — no mechanism branches on
which terrain a tile has or which resource is in it); Law 19 (every deleted parameter names its
read); Appendix B, Observer (no surface changes the model; the reachability test, not a demand that
every resource have a customer).

**And the thing this item must not drift back into.** A tile's characteristics are Law 2 TECHNOLOGY
primitives — drawn, declared, one writer, many readers — and the physical world is exactly what that
law exists to let in ("real-world primitives may be imported"). They are not to be minimised away,
derived from one another, or withheld until an economic mechanism asks for them. Every declared
resource is drawn on every tile from the first world, because a map that changes when a recipe is
added is not a map.
